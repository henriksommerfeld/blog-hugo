In early spring 2026 I bought my first mountain bike for recreational riding, at the age of 45. What bike I bought is in the title, but what I did to make the decision, might be more interesting.

{{<post-image image="bike-with-helmet.webp" alt="A black mountain bike with a red full-face helmet on the bar." />}}

## Research

The idea of taking up biking grew on me slowly, so I spent the winter figuring out what I wanted to get out of my riding.

In terms of *why*, I think *smiles per hour* is a good overall measurement to use. I’m not particularly fit, so getting some physical exercise was definitely part of the plan as well.

### My Location
I live at the foot of a mountain with a bike park at the top. Nothing is flat around here, but it’s also not exactly the Alps either. It's only about 200 metres of elevation from my home to the bike park.

Inspired by the fantastic bike-handling skills of people like [Chris Akrigg][1] and [Erin (Super Rider)][2], I was more drawn to mountain biking than road biking.

### E-bike or Acoustic
Given my location and life puzzle with small kids, I wanted to start my bike rides from home rather than loading the bike onto the car (which is usually taken by my wife) and driving to the bike park.

This brought me to the question of whether I should get a regular bike or an e-bike. So I walked from home up to the bike park at a decent pace to see how tired I'd be when I arrived. This wasn't entirely conclusive, but I started leaning towards an e-bike so I wouldn't be discouraged by losing too much energy just getting to the starting point of a lot of trails.

### Watching YouTube
To some extent, I found watching bike reviews on YouTube useful. I learned a lot of terminology, but there are also limitations to how useful these videos can be:

- Most bike "content" (disgusting word, but appropriate here) is advertising in some form.
- Reviewers review super-expensive configurations of every bike, so it doesn't always translate that well to what I'm considering buying.
- Reviewers (always from other countries) often review bikes that aren't available for me to buy where I live.

### Browsing Bike Store Websites

When I started to have a rough idea of what kind of bike I wanted, I started browsing the websites of stores I could see myself buying from, of course including my local bike shops, to see what was available and at what prices.

## Deciding Criteria

Looking back on my decision to buy the specific bike I did, these were the main factors.

### Manuals

Not all bike brands make information about how to service their bikes easily available on their websites. The best brand I found in this regard was Trek (among the bikes available in Swedish bike stores). They had exploded views of the entire bikes from many years ago, so I could buy a used bike and still be able to find spare parts and understand how they should be fitted. They also had manuals in many languages.

I cannot understand how this isn't a given for all brands. When I looked at Propain's website and it had a bunch of broken links and half the information in German despite choosing English, I felt discouraged from buying a bike from them. I gave feedback on this and suggested a tool for finding broken links. When I now visit their Tech Archive again, all links seem to work and I now link to their [Tech Archive](https://service.propain-bikes.com/en_GB/technical-archive/tech-archive) as a good example of how it should be. Great improvement!

### Local Bike Shop Repairability

In addition to having good manuals from Trek, their bikes are also repairable at two of my local bike shops. My goal is to do most maintenance myself, but I might screw up, and then it’s good to know I can get professional help. This is especially true for an e-bike, which I'd decided on by this point.

### Removable Battery

Since I wasn't sure I could store (and charge) the bike inside, I wanted a removable battery. In the end, I managed to make room in the basement, but it’s definitely something to consider. The Fuel+ still has two screws to unscrew to remove the battery, which is doable, but not great. Trek's Rail just has a key, like most commuter bikes. Obviously much better.

### Low vs High-Power E-Bike

I went with the low-power TQ-HPR60 motor with the 580 Wh battery. This was mostly based on gut feeling, but it turned out well. What appealed to me was the supposedly quiet motor and the fact that its appearance doesn't scream "E-BIKE!"

### Availability

Once I decided on the Trek Fuel+, which was new for 2026, I bought the cheapest configuration (EX 5) because that was what was available at the time. I also thought the olive green version looked cooler, but not available during a 10 percent discount period, so that made the decision for me.

## Hindsight

Now in late autumn with my bike is disassembled in the basement for the first real service, I can reflect on the outcome of this purchase.

### Pedal Assistance

I don't think I will regret getting an e-bike until I have an electrical failure, but I don’t regret getting a low-powered one either. My rider weight (fully dressed with shoes, a helmet and sometimes a backpack) is about 95 kg, so a higher-powered bike would also make sense. My bike's motor gives at most 350 W and 60 Nm of torque. Compared to the most powerful e-bike motors with 1,500 W and 150 Nm, there is a big difference.

Sure, there are boring steep sections where I would use more power if I had it, but typically I use power mode 2/3, which I've set to 224 W.

{{<post-image image="fuel-plus-power-2-3.webp" alt="Top of bike frame with a TQ display showing full battery level, user pedaling power 358W, bike assist 227W and power level 2 of 3">}}
My power: 358W. Bike's power: 227W
{{</post-image>}}

But here is the thing. I don’t do “e-bike rides”, I do bike rides with pedal assist. I appreciate the quietness and smoothness of the TQ motor.

One last thing about the pedal assist worth mentioning is that the battery goes down to 10% before it reduces assistance. For comparison, my cargo bike cuts the assistance level down to a minimum already at 20%, so I can't pedal my way up the hill to get home.

### Smiles per Hour
The bike has made me get off my butt more often to get a real workout 1-2 times a week, which is obviously a good thing. I also get mental relief when riding my bike. When focusing on going left or right of a rock in order not to fall, I cannot hear my kids whining. At that moment, they don't exist, and I find that very healthy. I've kept biking throughout the season, so I think that’s a strong enough indication that I enjoy it.
